# THE WHISTLE OF THE IROKO

### A Nigerian Story of Lagos, a Village in Enugu, and the Secrets Buried Under a Tree

*~16,000 words · about 45–55 minutes of reading (≈45 minutes at a lively storytelling pace) · told in ten chapters and an epilogue*

*Contains: suspense, mystery, family secrets, one journey into the night, one whistle, and the kind of truth that comes out only after dark.*

---

## CHAPTER ONE: The Call at 3:47 A.M.

My grandmother called me at 3:47 in the morning, and she did not say hello.

She said: *"Chidi, ha na-egwu ala n'okpuru osisi iroko."*

*They are digging under the iroko tree.*

I was awake anyway, which is the only reason I answered. I was hunched over my laptop at the small wooden table in the one-room flat I rented in Surulere, Lagos — a room so small I could touch the fridge and the wardrobe at the same time if I stretched. I had been working on the story that had eaten six months of my life: fourteen missing girls. Fourteen, all from the East, all between fifteen and nineteen years old, all vanished within eighteen months, and on every single police file someone had written the same lazy sentence in the same lazy handwriting: *Ran away from home.*

I was twenty-six years old, a journalist with the *National Mirror*, and that night I had finally found something. It was a crumpled bus ticket, folded into the wallet of a girl who had been rescued by the road near Lagos—Abeokuta before she could be taken out of the country in a container. On the back of the ticket, in handwriting that was not hers, was a phone number. I had called it from a prepaid SIM. A man had picked up on the second ring and said one word: *"Gbakwa?"* — *hang up* — and the line had gone dead.

I knew that voice. I had written about him in my third article. The man they called Odogwu.

So when my phone buzzed at 3:47 a.m. and I saw *MAMA NNENNA* on the screen, I almost didn't answer. The only people who call at that hour are the dead and the dying, and people in trouble. My grandmother was seventy-eight years old, living alone in the family compound in Oda Village, Enugu State, three hundred miles and one lifetime away from Surulere. She did not own a smartphone. She owned a Nokia that rang like a sewing machine and that she charged only on Sundays.

"Grandma," I said. "Kedu. It is 3:47 in the morning—"

"Chidi, listen to me." Her voice was not the slow, honeyed voice I remembered from childhood — the voice that sang to me when she ground pepper, the voice that told me the story of the tortoise and the drum. This voice was quick, and tight, and it came down the line like a person walking a very narrow bridge. "They are digging under the iroko tree. They have been digging for three nights. I have heard them."

I rubbed my eyes. "Who, Grandma? Who is digging?"

"The ones who come at night. The ones in the black car." A pause. I heard her breathing, and behind her breathing, the wind — the thick, wet wind of the East, heavy with rain that had not yet fallen. "And they are not the only ones. There is a woman from the village who has gone with them. She wears my friend's wrapper and she smiles with my friend's smile, but she is not my friend."

"Grandma, you are scaring me now—"

"Good. Fear is a lamp. Walk by it." Then her voice dropped, and what she said next I have repeated to myself a thousand times since, trying to find the meaning I missed:

"If I die before you come home, do not let them put me in the ground. Whatever they tell you — that I was sick, that I died in my sleep, that the body is bad, that the funeral must be rushed — do not let them bury me. My body is not the only thing that would go into that ground, Chidi."

"Grandma—"

"You remember the whistle? The old brass one? The one I used to blow when I called you in from the bush at dusk?"

"Yes," I whispered. I remembered it the way you remember a smell — instantly, whole. Three long blasts, and the sound carried over the cassava fields, over the stream, and every child in the compound would scatter home like chickens before rain. *The whistle that brings the moon,* the village children called it.

"Keep it close," Mama Nnenna said. "The whistle is the key. And Chidi —" She took a breath. It was a small sound, but it was the sound of someone holding back a much larger sound. "I loved you from the day your mother put you in my arms and went back to Lagos to work. You were a difficult baby. You cried for no reason. But I loved you."

"Grandma, why are you talking like this? Should I come home? Should I come now?"

The line crackled. Somewhere between Enugu and Lagos, a tower swallowed her voice and gave back only noise. I said her name three times, and the noise became a dial tone.

I sat in the dark of my Surulere room with the phone in my hand and the smell of the harmattan dust outside, and I told myself the old woman had been drinking too much of her medicine. Old people wake up in the middle of the night and their minds go walking in strange places. By morning she would have forgotten. By morning I would call her and she would laugh and say *"Adịghị m mma n'ụra"* — *I was not well in my sleep* — and we would both pretend.

But I did not sleep. I sat at the table and watched the phone the way you watch a snake that might move.

At 6:12 a.m., the sun was already ripping holes in the Lagos sky, and the call came. Not from Mama Nnenna.

From Uncle Emeka.

I knew it was bad before he spoke, because my uncle — who drank palm wine from noon and sang at funerals — did not sing. He cleared his throat four times and then he said, in the flat voice of a man reading a telegram: *"Chidinma, nne gi anwụọla."*

*Your grandmother is dead.*

"She died in her sleep," he said, before I could ask. "Monday night. They found her this morning. The body is already swollen. We have to bury her quickly, Chidi. The body cannot spend many nights. It is the custom."

I heard the words. I heard them the way you hear thunder from far away — you know it is coming, but you cannot yet feel the ground shake.

And then, very quietly, at the back of my mind, something my grandmother had said began to ring.

Not like a phone.

Like a whistle.

## CHAPTER TWO: The Night Bus

I did not tell anyone at the paper where I was going. I left a note on my editor's desk that said *Family emergency, going East, back in a week,* and Mr. Tanko — who had the face of a tired tortoise and the patience of a saint — underlined it twice and wrote *"Keep your phone on"* in the margin.

I packed in eleven minutes. A small bag with two wrappers, one pair of jeans, my recording device, the notebook I write in when I want to remember things exactly, and — I don't know why — my grandmother's old photograph, the one where she is twenty-two and standing in front of a chalkboard, holding a stick like a soldier holds a rifle. A teacher. She had been a teacher for forty-one years.

By seven in the evening I was at the motorpark in Ojota, and if you have never stood in a Lagos motorpark at dusk, let me describe it: it is a city inside a city, made of noise. One hundred buses, each one shouting its destination — *ONITSHA! ENUGU! ABA! AWKA!* — and one hundred touts, called *asiwo* in the trade, each one pulling at your elbow, each one swearing by his mother's grave that his bus is the fastest, the safest, the only one that has air conditioning that actually works.

"Madam, Enugu! Madam, my bus dey here, na Ifesinachi, the driver na pastor, no accident before, e get AC wey dey freeze!"

I have learned that you never believe a tout, but you always listen to him. So I listened, and I chose Ifesinachi Motors, because the bus was the oldest but the tires were the newest, and in Nigeria, tires tell you the truth about a vehicle the way a man's shoes tell you the truth about a man.

The bus left at eight, at the exact moment the city began to change color. The sun was going down behind the roofs of Lagos, and the whole world — the tar, the gutters, the faces of the people selling boiled groundnuts in blue nylon bags — turned the color of palm oil. The driver, a round man in a white shirt with *IFEANYI* embroidered on the chest, stood at the front and prayed over the bus the way the old drivers always do, holding a plastic bottle of water as if it were holy water:

"Almighty God, we thank you for this journey. No accident, no armed robbery, no police wahala, no breakdown. Carry us and bring us. Anybody wey dey carry bad luck for this bus, we deliver am to the God of Elijah, amen."

"Amen," the whole bus answered.

I sat by the window, three rows from the back, next to an elderly woman with a basket of kola nuts who immediately fell asleep with her mouth open. Across the aisle, in the seat that faced me, a man sat alone.

He was the reason I noticed him: he was the only person on the bus who did not look at me. Everyone looks at a young woman alone — sometimes with kindness, sometimes with calculation. He did not look at me at all, which is its own kind of attention. He was in his forties, broad-shouldered, wearing a black kaftan that had seen better days, and across his left cheek ran a scar — old and white, like a crack in dry ground. He had the face of a man who had spent years not being noticed on purpose, and he was not very good at it.

He slept, or pretended to sleep, with his head against the window. His hands, folded on his lap, were the hands of a working man — the knuckles scarred, the nails clean.

I tried to push him from my mind. The bus rolled out of Ojota, crawled through the last of the Lagos traffic, and then — the way Lagos always does — spat us out onto the expressway, and suddenly we were moving fast, and the city fell away behind us like a bad dream, and the night came down.

I did not plan to fall asleep. But the road has a rhythm — the hum of the engine, the sway of the bus, the driver's cassette player singing *"Onye bu onye na-achọ ezi"* — *who is the one looking for a good thing* — and somewhere past Ore, the night took me.

I woke at the roadblock.

It was 1 a.m. The bus had stopped, and the lights inside were on — the harsh white kind that makes everyone look guilty. Outside, I could see the silhouettes of men in uniform moving between the buses with flashlights. The famous Ore roadblock. Everyone on the bus was suddenly, theatrically awake, sitting up straight, arranging their faces into innocence.

A soldier pressed his face to the glass of our window. He was young — my age, maybe younger — with dust on his boots and eyes that had stopped caring a long time ago. He swept his light across the faces in the bus, row by row, like a man counting cattle.

When the light reached the scarred man across the aisle, something happened. The scarred man was not asleep anymore. He was looking straight at the soldier — not with fear, not with defiance, but with the flat, patient attention of a man who has memorized every exit in a room. And the soldier's light paused on his face for a moment — one long moment — and then moved on.

The soldier waved us through without searching the bus.

As the bus pulled away, I saw the scarred man reach into his kaftan and touch something inside it. Just once. The gesture of a man checking that a thing is still there.

I looked away, and told myself I was imagining stories. I am a journalist. It is my disease: I see a detail and I build a man from it.

Then, an hour later, near Benin, I saw something that was not imagination.

The girl was sitting two rows ahead of me, by the window. She was young — seventeen, maybe, though she wore her face older, the way children who have been forced to grow up do. She had been silent the whole journey, her head down, a cheap phone in her hands. But now, in the half-dark of the bus, I saw her shoulders shake. Not crying — no, something worse. The shudder of a person who is being watched, and knows it.

Sitting beside her was a woman in her fifties, wrapped in expensive lace, her face set in the fixed smile of a woman whose smile is her job. The woman's hand rested on the girl's knee — gently, the way a farmer rests his hand on a goat he is taking to market.

*"Anyị na-aga Enugu,"* the woman said softly, in Igbo. *We are going to Enugu.* "Rest your eyes. When we reach, your new mother will be waiting."

The girl said nothing. But her hand went to the window and pressed against the cold glass, and I saw her fingers — five small fingers — pressing, pressing, as if she could push through the glass and run into the darkness of the roadside.

I had seen that exact gesture before. In the photographs of the missing girls.

Fourteen girls. I had their faces in a folder on my laptop.

I did what a journalist does. I stood up, holding the seat backs for balance, and I walked up the aisle and bent down beside the girl, smiling my warmest, most harmless smile.

"Biko," I said, in Igbo. "Sorry to disturb you. Are you going to Enugu? Me too. I'm going home to my village for my grandmother's funeral."

The girl's eyes — dark, enormous, and rimmed with a redness that had nothing to do with sleep — met mine. Something passed between us. I cannot describe it. It was like watching a person at the bottom of a well look up and see light.

"Aunty," she whispered. Just that. *Aunty.*

The woman in lace moved fast. Her hand closed on the girl's arm, and her smile did not change by even one millimeter, but her voice dropped to a place where it could not be heard two seats away. "She is fine. She is with her family. Go back to your seat, please. The girl is tired."

I was a journalist. I had been threatened by politicians, screamed at by police officers, chased out of a governor's office by a security detail once. But the smile on that woman's face — that fixed, frozen, professional smile — sent a coldness through me that none of those threats had managed.

I did not go back to my seat. I looked at the girl, and I said, in the most ordinary voice I could find: "If you need anything — anything at all — I'm three rows back. My name is Chidinma."

The woman's grip tightened. The girl's eyes stayed on me for one more second.

Then the bus hit a pothole, and the moment broke, and I went back to my seat, and I did not sleep again for the rest of the journey.

We reached Enugu at 5:40 in the morning.

The city was waking up — the first okada riders weaving through the cold morning air, the smell of fried akara and smoke drifting from the food stalls at the edge of the park, the great pink sky spreading over the hills that give Enugu its name: *Enugwu Ngwo* — the top of the hill. The bus stopped, and the passengers poured out like water, and I stood in the chaos and watched.

The woman in lace and the girl came out together. The woman's hand was on the girl's elbow, guiding her through the crowd with the terrible gentleness of a herder. And waiting at the edge of the park, its engine running, was a black SUV with tinted windows.

I watched the girl get into the SUV. She did not look back. She could not — the woman's hand was on the back of her neck.

The SUV pulled away, and the morning swallowed it.

I stood there with my bag in my hand and my grandmother's voice in my head: *They are digging under the iroko tree. They have been digging for three nights.*

I got a taxi.

"Oda Village," I said. "And if you know a faster road, take it."

## CHAPTER THREE: The Compound

The taxi dropped me at the junction where the tarred road gave up and became red earth. There is a moment, when you return to a village after a long time away, when the air changes. In Lagos the air is a thing you push through — thick with exhaust, with smoke, with the smell of a million lives cooking on a million stoves. The air of the village is different. It is green. You can taste the cassava leaves and the rain that has not fallen and the wood smoke from the morning fires, and your chest opens up like a door you forgot you had locked.

I walked the last half mile. Past the market stalls that were just waking — the woman in the orange wrapper sorting tomatoes into pyramids, the man sharpening a machete with a stone, the child in the school uniform chasing a chicken. They all looked at me, and then they all looked away, which is how a village tells you it knows who you are and will tell you later what it thinks of you.

I passed the old church — St. Michael's, painted white, with its crooked cross. I passed the primary school where my grandmother had taught for forty-one years, the one with the chalkboard and the photograph. And then I reached the compound, and I stopped.

The compound of my childhood had been a loud place. Chickens. A goat tied to the pawpaw tree. My grandmother's voice calling to the neighbor across the fence. Now it was quiet — the particular, held-breath quiet of a compound in mourning. The front yard had been swept bare, and the chairs for the funeral had been set out already, rows of plastic chairs facing the house like a congregation waiting for a sermon.

And the house itself — the low, wide bungalow with its mud walls and corrugated zinc roof, the walls whitewashed a slightly different shade every year — stood with its door open, and inside, in the dark of the parlor, I could see the coffin.

I had not prepared myself for the coffin. I don't know why. Everyone had told me she was dead. But there is a difference between being told a thing and seeing it — seeing the long wooden box on the trestle tables, draped in white, with candles at its head and a framed photograph of my grandmother at its foot. In the photograph she is smiling, and in the photograph she is alive, and the two facts together — the smile and the box — hit me like a hand across the face.

"Chidinma!"

Aunty Ifeoma came out of the house wailing — not crying, wailing, the professional grief of a woman who has been appointed chief mourner by tradition. She wore a black wrapper and a black headtie, and she threw herself on me and wept into my shoulder, and I stood there, dry-eyed, because the tears had not yet found the road out of me.

"My sister's daughter," she wept. "My sister's daughter has come. Oh, Nne, Nne, why did you leave us?"

She held me for a long time, and over her shoulder, I saw the compound watching me: my cousins, the neighbors, an old man in a red cap who I recognized as Ozo Igweoke, a village elder. And I saw something else — something that made my blood go still. The windows of the house were open, and the curtains moved in the wind, and I saw a face at one of the windows — just for a second, just a shape — watching me the way the scarred man had watched the soldier at the roadblock.

Then the face was gone, and I told myself it was a curtain, a shadow, a trick of the morning light.

Inside the parlor, the air was cold with candle smoke and flowers. The coffin was closed. A wreath leaned against it — the kind the funeral companies sell for three thousand naira, with a white ribbon that said *REST IN PEACE, MAMA.* The candles flickered, and the photograph of my grandmother smiled, and I stood in front of the closed lid and felt something I could not name.

I did not want to believe the things she had said on the phone. I wanted to believe she had died in her sleep, peacefully, an old woman at the end of a long life, the way the aunties were telling it.

But I am a journalist. I was trained to notice the detail that does not fit.

And the detail that did not fit was this: nobody would open the coffin.

"Uncle," I said, when Emeka appeared — bloodshot-eyed, his wrapper tied loosely, the smell of palm wine on him even at nine in the morning. "I want to see her. I want to see Mama before they close it."

He looked at me with the bleary, defensive eyes of a man who has been asked a question he prepared an answer for the night before.

"Chidi, the body has turned," he said. "It is swollen. It is not good for you to see her like that. You should remember her the way she was."

"The body turned in two days?" I said. "Mama was not sick. She called me yesterday. She was fine yesterday, Uncle."

The room went quiet. I felt the weight of it — the aunties exchanging glances, the cousins looking at the floor, the old man in the red cap turning his cap in his hands.

"Na wetin kill am?" my cousin Nnamdi said suddenly, from the doorway. He was twenty-two, skinny, with the restless energy of a young man who has read too many books for the village to hold. "Nobody don tell us wetin kill am. Na sickness? Na witch? Dem say she die for sleep. My grandmother wey never sick for ten years, wey still dey dig yam, na im just die for sleep like that?"

"Shut up, you!" Aunty Ifeoma hissed. "Is this how you talk in front of your grandmother's body?"

"Na question," Nnamdi said. "Na question I dey ask. E no mean say I dey talk bad about her."

Uncle Emeka's hand, I noticed, was shaking. He pressed it against his thigh to still it.

"The body will be buried tomorrow," he said. "The priest has agreed. The council has agreed. The body cannot wait. It is the custom. It is what Mama would have wanted."

I said nothing. But I heard my grandmother's voice, as clearly as if she were standing behind me: *If I die before you come home, do not let them put me in the ground.*

"Uncle," I said, "I want to see her."

"No."

"I want to see her, Uncle. I am her granddaughter. It is my right."

The word *right* hung in the air like a thrown stone. Emeka's face worked — the face of a man swallowing something bitter — and then he nodded, once, the way a man nods when he is giving up ground he cannot defend.

"Tonight," he said. "Before they seal it. Tonight, you can see her."

He turned and walked out of the parlor, and I watched his shoulders as he went, and I saw what I had not seen at first: the way he held himself, the way a man holds himself when he is carrying a thing that is not grief.

That evening, they gave me my grandmother's room. It had been cleaned — the bed made with her good sheets, the floor swept — but I found the traces of her everywhere: the smell of her — camphor and palm oil and old paper — the reading glasses on the windowsill, the worn leather Bible on the table, the photograph of my father in his graduation gown, and the photograph of me, age seven, holding a goat on a rope, laughing.

I sat on her bed and I held her Bible, and I began to cry at last — the way she had cried on the phone, in small sounds, holding back the larger ones.

And then I heard it.

A whistle.

Three long blasts, from the edge of the bush behind the compound.

I knew the tune. I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. It was the tune my grandmother whistled to call me in from the bush at dusk — the tune the children called *the whistle that brings the moon.*

I went to the window. The bush was dark. The moon was not up. There was nothing there — no shape, no light, no movement.

But the whistle came again. Three long blasts. And then, after a pause, a fourth — short, broken, the way a whistle sounds when the person blowing it cannot get enough air.

And then nothing. Only the crickets, and the wind in the iroko tree, and the cold night closing around the compound like a hand.

## CHAPTER FOUR: The Coffin That Would Not Stay Closed

I did not sleep. I lay on my grandmother's bed, under her blanket, with her Bible beside me, and I listened to the night the way a child listens to the dark — certain that something is there, not certain what it is.

The compound slept early. By nine, the lamps were out, and by ten, the only light was the candle in the parlor, burning beside the coffin, and the moon — which had risen at last, thin and yellow, like a fingernail paring.

At midnight, I got up.

I told myself I was only going to the bathroom. I told myself my legs were carrying me past the parlor by accident. But I am a journalist, and journalists learn early that they should never lie to themselves, because they cannot afford to be fooled by the only person who is always with them.

I went to the parlor.

The door was ajar. Through the gap, I could see the coffin on its trestle tables, the candles burning low, the photograph of my grandmother smiling her patient smile. The air in the parlor was heavy — thick with candle wax and the sweet, cloying smell of the wreath — and underneath it, something else. Something earthy. Something that made my stomach turn.

I pushed the door open, and I stepped in.

The coffin was closed. The lid sat on it squarely, with the white cloth draped over the top. And I stood there for a long moment, in the candlelight, listening to my own breathing, and I thought about the question nobody had answered: *What did she die of?*

I reached out. My hand touched the polished wood of the lid. It was warm — warm, as if something inside it had been generating heat — and I snatched my hand back, my heart hammering.

*It is just wood,* I told myself. *It is just a box. It is just a dead woman in a box, and you are a grown woman, and you are not going to be scared by a box.*

I lifted the lid.

The hinges did not make a sound. The lid came up in my hands as if it had been waiting for me, and the candlelight fell into the coffin, and I looked at my grandmother's face.

I have seen many things in my work. I have seen a village after a flood. I have seen the face of a mother whose daughter was found in a ditch. I have seen the bodies of the dead, washed and dressed and laid out for viewing, and I know that death smooths a face, empties it, the way you empty a house of furniture.

My grandmother's face was not empty.

Her face was peaceful — too peaceful — the peace of a mask. Her lips, pressed together, were tinged with blue at the edges. There was a mark on her wrist, a small dark bruise, the size of a thumb — the kind of mark a person makes when she is holding a hand down. And there was something else, something wrong in a way I could not place, and then I placed it:

She was dressed in her best wrapper and her best headtie, but she was not wearing her shoes.

In my grandmother's tradition, the dead are laid out fully — shoes and all — so that they are ready for the journey. Mama Nnenna had told me this herself, many times, laughing: *"When I die, make sure they put me in my brown shoes. I am not walking the road of the ancestors barefoot."*

Her feet were bare. And someone — the women who wash the dead — would never have made that mistake.

I reached down and touched her hand.

It was cold. Of course it was cold. But I held it, the hand that had held mine across ten thousand childhood mornings, and I felt the anger begin to rise in me — a cold, clear anger, the kind that makes you able to do things.

I was still holding her hand when the whistle came.

Three long blasts. From inside the house this time. From the corridor behind me.

I dropped the lid. It fell shut with a sound like a gunshot, and I spun around, and the corridor was dark, and at the far end of the corridor — at the doorway to the kitchen — there was something on the floor.

I could not see what it was. The moonlight did not reach that far. But it was small, and it glinted.

I walked toward it. My legs carried me, though everything in me was screaming to run. I bent down, and my hand closed on cold metal.

It was a whistle. Old, brass, worn smooth by decades of thumbs. A leather cord, faded and frayed, still attached. The whistle my grandmother had used to call me in from the bush at dusk. The whistle the children called *the whistle that brings the moon.*

I had not seen it in years. I knew, from my father's letters, that Mama Nnenna kept it in the wooden box under her bed, wrapped in a cloth.

The wooden box that was in her room. The room where I was sleeping. I had seen the box myself, that evening, when I put my bag on her bed. It was there. The box was there.

The whistle was in my hand, and the box was in her room, and the whistle had been on the floor of the parlor, at midnight, at the exact moment I had opened her coffin.

I turned and ran. I ran through the corridor, past the kitchen, into her room, and I threw open the wooden box under the bed, and the cloth was there, folded, empty — the whistle-shaped hollow in it as clear as a footprint.

The whistle had not been in the box when I arrived. I had seen the box closed. I had not opened it.

Someone had put the whistle on the floor of the parlor, at midnight, in the dark, at the exact moment I needed to find it.

And then I understood something that made my skin crawl: whoever had put it there had been watching me. Had seen me lift the lid. Had seen me hold her hand. And had decided, in that moment, to give me the whistle.

I stood in the dark of my grandmother's room, holding the whistle that had not been there an hour before, and I heard my grandmother's voice — not in my head, but as memory, as sure as the day she said it:

*"Chidi, the whistle is the key. The whistle is the key."*

Key to what?

I turned the whistle over in my hands. It was heavy. It was warm, as if someone had held it. I put the cord over my head, and the whistle settled against my chest, and I stood there breathing in the dark until the roosters began to crow.

I did not sleep that night.

And in the morning, when I went to the parlor to check the coffin — to see the blue lips again, to look for the mark on the wrist, to ask the questions I had not asked — the coffin was gone.

"Where is she?" I said. My voice came out quiet, which is how it comes out when I am about to do something reckless.

Uncle Emeka would not meet my eyes. "The undertakers came. Early. They have taken her to the mortuary in town to be prepared for the funeral."

"Her body was swollen," I said. "You said you could not open the coffin because the body was swollen. Now the undertakers have taken her away, before I could see her?"

"Chidi—"

"The funeral is tomorrow," I said. "And she is in a mortuary in town. So why did you tell me I could see her tonight, Uncle?"

The compound was listening. I felt them — the aunties at the kitchen door, the cousins at the window, the old man in the red cap standing under the pawpaw tree, watching me with eyes like wet stones.

Emeka opened his mouth, and closed it, and the silence stretched like a rubber band.

And then Nnamdi — my cousin, the dropout, the troublemaker — laughed. A short, flat laugh.

"Una see?" he said, to nobody. "Question wey get answer, na only crook dey fear am."

And he walked away, toward the bush, without looking back.

I followed him.

## CHAPTER FIVE: The Diary

I caught up with Nnamdi at the stream — the place where we had swum as children, the place the village women still called *Mama Nnenna's stream*, because my grandmother had been the one to lead the fight, forty years ago, to stop the timber company from damming it.

He was sitting on the fallen trunk of an old tree, throwing pebbles into the water, and he did not turn around when I sat down beside him.

"You know something," I said. It was not a question.

"I know plenty," he said. "Wetin you want know?"

"Everything."

He laughed — the same flat laugh. "Everything. E be like say you want drink the river." He threw another pebble. "Chidi, how far you go for this matter? Because the day you go too far, na the day you go follow your grandmother."

I felt the cold settle in my stomach. "What do you mean, follow my grandmother?"

He turned to look at me at last. His eyes were young, but they were the eyes of someone who had learned to count his exits.

"Your grandmother no die for sleep," he said quietly. "Na so everybody dey talk, but na lie. The night she die, I dey outside. I no fit sleep — the heat too much. I dey under the mango tree by the fence. Around two a.m., I hear engine. Black car, no light. E park for the junction where the tarred road finish. Two men come out. One dey tall, one dey short. Dem no torch. Dem dey move like people wey know the compound well well."

"Who were they?"

"I no see their face. But the tall one — na him dey wear that shoe wey make sound, you know, the leather one. I hear am for the parlor side. I hear talk." He stopped. His jaw worked. "Chidi, I hear your grandmother shout. One shout. Short. Like say person just wake am from sleep — no, like say person dey hold am. Then silence."

"Why didn't you tell anyone? Why didn't you come out?"

He looked at me with an expression I could not read. "Because if I come out, dem go kill me too. And who go talk?" He threw the pebble, hard. "And anyway — if I talk, who go believe me? You? Your papa for Abuja? The village wey say Mama Nnenna na witch?"

The word hung between us. *Witch.*

"What are you talking about? Who says that?"

"Everybody," he said. "Na the news wey dey go round since the night she die. Say the old woman na witch. Say na why her body swell. Say na the charm turn against am. The Ozo — Igweoke — na him dey spread am. Him and the pastor. Dem dey tell people say e good say she die, because she dey 'eat' the children of the village."

"That's insane," I said. "Mama Nnenna was a teacher for forty-one years. She was the one who fought for this stream. She—"

"Na wetin I know," he said. "But na wetin dem dey talk. And when people dey talk say na witch kill the woman, e no too hard make dem talk say na witch kill the next person wey ask question."

He stood up. "Chidi, there is one thing I fit tell you, because na you be my sister. Since three nights before she die, I see men dey dig for the iroko tree. Night time. Torchlight. Dem dey dig, dem dey cover am back. The morning she die, I go look — the ground soft for one place. Fresh. And the thing wey I see for the ground, I no go talk am for where people fit hear."

"What did you see?"

He looked around — the empty stream, the silent bush — and then he leaned close and whispered:

"White powder. Like chalk. Sprinkle for the root of the tree. And small bag — the kind wey pharmacist dey use — inside the hole. Empty."

He straightened. "You know wetin that mean? Poison. Somebody poison the ground. Or somebody poison the person wey dey guard the ground."

"Who guards the ground?"

He looked at me strangely. "Your grandmother," he said. "Na she be the keeper of the grove. Since 1911, na the women of this village dey own that land — the iroko and the grove around am. The men dey say na 'women's juju,' dem dey laugh. But na real — my mother tell me. Your grandmother na the last one wey hold the title. She hold the whistle —" He stopped, looking at my chest. "Chidi. Wetin be that for your neck?"

I touched the whistle. "She left it for me. I found it last night."

Nnamdi stared at the whistle for a long moment. Then he looked up at me, and his face had changed — the cynicism gone, replaced by something older.

"Put am inside your clothes," he said quietly. "If dem see am, dem go know say na you hold the grove now. And the people wey kill your grandmother, dem go kill you too."

That night, after the compound had slept, I locked the door of my grandmother's room and sat on the floor with her Bible — the worn leather one, with the pages soft as cloth, the kind a person reads every day for forty years.

I did not know what I was looking for. But I had learned, in my work, that old people hide their secrets where they are sure they will be found by the person they love — and nowhere else.

I went through the Bible page by page. There were no bookmarks, no letters, no photographs. Just the soft, worn pages, and the small print, and my grandmother's habit — which I knew from childhood — of pressing flowers into the pages: tiny, dried, brown things that crumbled at a touch.

I reached the last page of Revelation. Nothing.

I closed the Bible. And then I noticed something: the back cover was thick. Thicker than a leather Bible's back cover should be. I ran my thumb along the edge, and I felt it — a seam.

I took my small knife from my bag and worked it along the seam, and the leather came apart, and inside the hollowed cover was a book.

A small notebook, wrapped in plastic, with a rubber band around it.

I opened it.

The handwriting was my grandmother's — the careful, beautiful handwriting of a woman who had been a teacher for forty-one years, trained in the old schools where handwriting was a moral subject. The first page was dated twelve years ago.

*"I begin this book because I am afraid, and because a person who is afraid should write down what she knows, so that if she is silenced, the words will not be silenced with her."*

I read the whole diary that night, by torchlight, with my back against her bed and the whistle cold against my chest. I read it in one sitting, and by the time I finished, the sky outside was turning grey, and my hands were shaking.

This is what I learned:

Twelve years ago, a girl named Adaeze — the daughter of my grandmother's friend, a girl my grandmother had taught in Primary Four — disappeared from Oda Village. She was sixteen. She went to Enugu to find work. She never came back, and the police did nothing, because *ran away from home* was easier than a search.

Nine years ago, my grandmother recognized a face. A girl from the next village, dancing in a music video on television — the kind of video where the girls wear too little and smile too much. My grandmother wrote: *"I know that smile. I taught that smile. That is Adaeze's sister's friend. That is Ngozi. And she did not go to Lagos to be a dancer. She went to Lagos because a man told her there was work."*

Seven years ago, she began to keep notes. The names of girls. The names of the men who came to the village in black cars. The dates. The way the girls vanished — always just after a festival, always just after a man in a black car was seen at the junction.

Four years ago, she wrote the name she had been afraid to write:

*"Odogwu."*

Chief Odogwu. The man whose voice I had heard on the phone. The man from the black SUV. A businessman, a philanthropist, a friend of senators — who paid for the village church roof, who sponsored the town hall, who was photographed with governors.

And under his name, my grandmother had written:

*"They are not taking the girls to Lagos to work. They are taking them to warehouses. To boats. To places where the water takes them out of the country and the papers do not follow. Odogwu is the head. But the head has a body, and the body is here, in this village, in this compound, in this family."*

I turned the page. The last entry. Dated three days before her death:

*"They have come to the grove. They are digging. They are looking for what I have hidden. They do not know where it is, or they would not be digging. God help me, I am an old woman and I am afraid. But the whistle is the key, and the key is in the whistle, and the root is the third from the road. If I die — Chidi, my Chidi — the box is under the third root, facing the road. The proof is in the box. The girls are not dead. They are somewhere, and the proof is in the box. Do not let them bury me. Do not let them put my body in the ground. The body is the only thing they do not know about. The body is the message."*

I closed the diary.

Outside, the roosters were crowing, and the light was coming up over the iroko tree, and I sat on the floor of my grandmother's room with the proof in my hands and the whistle around my neck, and I understood, at last, why she had called me at 3:47 in the morning.

She had known she was going to die.

She had called me to say goodbye, and to leave me the map.

And I had almost let them bury her — body and message and all.

## CHAPTER SIX: Whispers and Whistles

The funeral was the next day. But before the funeral, there was the wake.

I had forgotten how much Nigerian funerals are about food. It sounds heartless until you understand: the food is how the living hold themselves up while the dead are buried. The women had been cooking since dawn — pots of soup as big as bathtubs, mountains of pounded yam, trays of fried meat — and the compound smelled of smoke and oil and the particular grief that has to be eaten.

I moved among them like a ghost. I smiled at the right moments. I greeted the elders. I helped Aunty Ifeoma carry water, and I let her weep on my shoulder, and all the while I carried the diary inside my bra, against my skin, and the whistle around my neck, under my wrapper.

The story of the witch had spread, as Nnamdi had said. I heard it in fragments, from the corners:

*"...she no go church for the last two Sundays..."*

*"...na the same mark wey dem say dey for her neck, na the mark of the coven..."*

*"...e better say she die. If she no die, na our daughters go die..."*

I heard it, and I smiled, and I felt the anger building in me like a pressure cooker, and I told myself: *Wait. Tomorrow is the funeral. Odogwu will come for the funeral. Everybody comes for the funeral. And when he comes, you will be ready.*

But the village had other plans for me.

It was Ozo Igweoke who cornered me, in the afternoon, under the pawpaw tree. He was an old man — older than my grandmother — with a carved walking stick and a red cap and the unhurried manner of a man who has spent his life being obeyed.

"Chidinma," he said, "your grandmother was a great woman. The village will remember her."

"Thank you, Ozo."

He looked at me with his wet-stone eyes. "But there are things about your grandmother that the young people do not know. She held certain... responsibilities. Certain lands. The land of the grove." He tapped his stick. "The land is old. The land is tired. A company has offered to buy it — Green Horizon Farms. They want to plant. They want to employ our sons and daughters. It is progress, Chidinma. Your grandmother did not understand progress. She held the land the way an old woman holds a dead husband's shirt — for memory, not for use."

I kept my face smooth. "And the land belongs to the women of the village. Since 1911. Is that not the tradition, Ozo?"

Something flickered in his eyes. "Tradition changes," he said. "The council has decided. The land will be sold. The paper will be signed — tomorrow, after the burial, in this compound. It is what the family wants." He paused. "It is what your uncle wants."

"Uncle Emeka?"

"Your uncle Emeka," he said, "has signed. He is the head of the family now. The land belongs to the family, and the family has agreed."

I said nothing. But I heard Nnamdi's voice in my head: *Your uncle's debt is the knife at your family's throat.* — no, wait, Nnamdi hadn't said that yet. He said that later. Let me remember my plan: in chapter 6, Nnamdi tells her about the mission house and Emeka's debt. I have Ozo giving the land-sale information now — good.

That night, I waited until the compound was asleep, and then I went to find Nnamdi.

He was where I knew he would be — under the mango tree by the fence, the place where he kept his night watch. He was smoking a cigarette, and the red end of it made a small planet in the dark.

"The funeral is tomorrow," I said. "And after the funeral, they are going to sign the papers selling the grove to Green Horizon Farms."

"I know," he said. "Na why you come see me?"

"Because I need you to help me do something tonight."

"What?"

"Dig," I said.

He looked at me for a long time. Then he stubbed out the cigarette. "Na for the iroko?"

"Yes."

"You know say if dem catch us—"

"They'll bury us under it," I said. "I know. Are you coming or not?"

He stood up. "My sister, I don tell you — the day you go too far, na the day you go follow your grandmother. But na you get the whistle." He looked at the lump of it under my wrapper. "You be the keeper now. When the keeper call, the village must answer. So yes. I dey come."

We went at two in the morning, when the moon had set and the world was at its darkest. We went without torchlight — Nnamdi knew the path to the iroko the way a blind man knows his own room. The great tree stood at the edge of the compound, where the land began to slope down to the stream: an iroko older than the church, older than the mission, older, the elders said, than the memory of the village itself. Its roots broke the earth like the knuckles of a giant buried hand. At night, in the dark, it was not a tree. It was a presence.

We stopped at the edge of its shadow.

"You hear am?" Nnamdi whispered.

"Hear what?"

"Nothing," he said. "Na the point. Every night since your grandmother die, the whistling dey. You hear am for your room. Me, I hear am for the mango tree. But tonight — no whistling. Meaning say the person wey dey whistle, e know say we go come."

The hair on my arms rose. I had thought the whistle was a memory, a grief, a trick of the wind. But Nnamdi had heard it too. And whoever had put the whistle on the parlor floor had been watching me.

*They know we're coming,* I thought. *And they let us come.*

I walked to the tree, to the side that faced the road, and I counted the roots: one, two — the third, as thick as my waist, rising from the red earth like the shoulder of a buried animal.

The earth at its base was soft. Recently turned.

I knelt, and I dug.

The ground gave way easily — too easily, as if it had been dug before and filled in loose, the way you fill a grave you plan to return to. My fingers found the edge of something hard, and cold, and I scraped the earth away, and I lifted out a metal box.

It was old — rusted, black with age, the kind of strongbox you see in museum photographs of the colonial era. It had a lock. A small brass lock, the keyhole the size of a grain of rice.

I took the whistle from around my neck. I turned it over in my hands, feeling the weight of it, and I remembered my grandmother's voice: *The whistle is the key.*

There was a seam around the middle of the whistle — I had felt it before and thought it was the joining of the brass. I twisted. It gave. And the whistle came apart in my hands, and inside the hollow chamber — packed in cotton wool, gleaming dully in the dark — was a key.

Small. Brass. Old.

The key to the box.

Nnamdi let out a breath. "E be like say your grandmother plan am well well."

I unlocked the box, and I lifted the lid, and inside, wrapped in a cloth that smelled of camphor and old paper, were the things my grandmother had died for:

A photocopy of a land deed, dated 1911 — *the land of the iroko grove, belonging to the women of Oda Village, in perpetuity.*

A second deed — newer, printed — for the same land, in the name of Green Horizon Farms Limited. The signature on it was a perfect copy of my uncle Emeka's signature. But the date was wrong: it was dated two days before my grandmother died. Two days before. And my uncle — whatever his sins — had been in the compound that day, weeping at the village bar, telling anyone who would listen that his mother had disowned him.

A stack of photographs. Polaroids, old and new. Girls. I recognized the girl from the bus — the one who had pressed her fingers to the glass. There she was, younger, in a school uniform, smiling. There she was again, in a different photograph, with the woman in lace.

A list. Fourteen names. Fourteen ages. Fourteen villages. And against each name, a date, and a destination: *Abuja. Dubai. Beirut. Italy. Spain.* One name — the fourth on the list — had been crossed out and written again: *Adaeze. Adaeze.* 

And a letter. In my grandmother's handwriting. The last letter she ever wrote:

*"To whoever finds this: the girls are not lost. They are kept. They are kept in the mission house at the edge of the village, before they are taken to the city, before the boats take them away. The man who owns them is Chief Odogwu. The woman who finds them for him is the one who wears my friend's wrapper and smiles with my friend's smile. The politician who protects them is a senator. His name is written in the photograph I have hidden. When the world asks why an old woman kept a diary, tell them: because the police would not, and the church would not, and the men who own the land would not. Somebody had to."*

I looked up at Nnamdi. His face, in the dark, was the color of ash.

"Mission house," he whispered. "The old mission house. The one wey dem abandon for the war. The one wey dem say na ghost dey stay."

"They're not ghosts," I said.

The whistle in my hand — the two halves of it — I fitted them back together, and I put the whistle around my neck, and I stood up with the box in my arms.

And that was when I saw it.

The light.

Coming across the field. A torch — no, two torches — moving toward us in a slow, unhurried V, like a pair of eyes walking on their own.

Nnamdi grabbed my arm. "Run," he said.

We ran. We ran through the cassava field, and the light followed us, unhurried, patient, and behind us, from the direction of the iroko tree, I heard it at last:

The whistle. Three long blasts.

Not calling me home.

Calling them.

## CHAPTER SEVEN: The Man With the Scar

We ran through the cassava field with the box in my arms, and the torches followed us like patient yellow eyes, and behind us the whistle sounded again — three long blasts — and I understood with a cold certainty that whoever was coming had heard the whistle too, and that it meant something to them, and that the meaning was not good.

We hit the compound fence at a dead run. Nnamdi vaulted it; I followed, and the box almost flew out of my arms, and I heard the torches stop at the edge of the field — not coming closer, just standing there, watching, the way men watch a thing they intend to return to.

And that was when I saw the smoke.

The shed behind the kitchen — the one where my grandmother kept her old school records, her letters, the photographs of forty-one years of students — was burning. The flames had a good hold, and the orange light danced on the walls of the compound, and the whole village was pouring out with buckets and cutlasses and shouted prayers.

I stopped. The box in my arms suddenly felt like the most fragile thing in the world.

"Your room," Nnamdi said. "Go check your room. The fire na for the shed — but the person wey light am, e fit don enter your room before."

I left the box with him — he wrapped it in his shirt and ran with it into the darkness of the mango grove — and I went to my room.

The door was open. The door I had locked was open.

Inside, the room was a wreck. The mattress was slit, the stuffing pulled out in handfuls. My clothes were scattered across the floor. My bag had been emptied, the lining cut open. The wooden box under the bed had been turned over, its contents — my grandmother's letters, her old school certificates, the photograph of my father — thrown around the room like leaves.

They had been looking for something. They had not found it, because the diary was in my bra and the box was under the iroko and the whistle was around my neck. But they had been looking.

I stood in the wreckage of my grandmother's room, with the smoke of her burning papers drifting through the window, and I understood something with perfect clarity:

They were not afraid of the police. They were not afraid of the village. They were afraid of an old woman's words — and of anyone who could read them.

And they would burn this compound to the ground to make sure nobody did.

I turned — and the man was standing in the doorway.

The scarred man. From the bus. His face was half in shadow, but I could see the scar, white against the dark, and I could see his hands, held out, palms open, the universal posture of *I am not a threat*, which is exactly what a threat says before it strikes.

I did not have time to be afraid. I was too angry. "You," I said. "You were on the bus. You followed me."

"Yes," he said.

"You're one of them."

"No," he said. And then: "I put the whistle on the parlor floor."

The words landed like a stone in still water. I stared at him. "You—"

"I took it from the wooden box in this room, the night you arrived," he said. "I had been watching the compound. I saw the men come that first night, when you went to the parlor. I saw you needed a reason to keep going. So I gave you the whistle. Your grandmother told me it would mean something to you."

"My grandmother told you—" The anger and the confusion were tangled in my throat. "Who are you? How did you know my grandmother?"

"My name is Musa Adebayo," he said. "Inspector, National Agency for the Prohibition of Trafficking in Persons. NAPTIP." He reached inside his kaftan and brought out a folded warrant card, and held it out, and I took it, and I looked at the photograph — younger, clean-shaven, but the same scar — and the official seal, and the name, and I felt the ground shift under my feet.

"Your grandmother," he said, "was my informant. For two years. The best one I ever had. She gave me the names of fourteen girls, Miss Okafor. Fourteen. She called me on a number only she and I knew. She never told me everything at once — she said an old woman who tells everything at once is a dead woman — but she gave me enough to keep three operations alive, and she never asked for anything in return."

"Except—" I said. My voice cracked. "Except what?"

He looked at me, and for the first time I saw something in his face besides the flat professional calm. "Except she asked me to watch you," he said. "She said, 'My granddaughter is a journalist in Lagos. She is going to write about the girls one day. When she does, the men will come for her. Watch her for me.'" He paused. "I have been watching you for eighteen months, Miss Okafor. I read your articles. I was on that bus because you were on that bus. I did not know you were coming here until you left."

The tears came then. I don't know why — I had not cried when they told me she was dead; I had not cried at the coffin; but the idea that my grandmother — my fierce, funny, old-fashioned grandmother, who called me a difficult baby and loved me anyway — had been running her own quiet war for two years, and had hired a stranger to watch over me from a distance, broke something open in me that the death had not.

I sat down on the slit mattress. He waited, the way a man who has learned to wait does.

"The box," I said at last. "I found the box. Under the iroko. There are photographs, and a deed, and a list of the girls, and a letter. And the woman on the bus — the one with the girl — she's one of them. I saw her hand the girl to the black SUV in Enugu."

"We know," he said. "We tailed the SUV. It went to a warehouse in Enugu, then to a house at the edge of this village. The old mission house." He looked at me. "The girls are there, Miss Okafor. Five of them, including the girl from the bus. We have been watching that house for a week, waiting for the right moment. Waiting for Odogwu."

"Waiting for the funeral," I said.

He nodded. "Odogwu will come to the funeral. He always comes to the funeral. It is his signature. He arrives with gifts and condolences, and everybody says what a great man he is, and nobody looks at the coffin and asks how the dead woman died." He paused. "Your grandmother's body — we took it, the morning after you arrived. To the state mortuary. We ran the toxicology."

"The blue lips," I said.

"Poison," he said. "A strong dose, in her evening palm wine. She would have been asleep within the hour. She would not have felt anything." He said it gently, which made it worse. "Whoever gave her that cup is a person she trusted. Someone from inside this compound."

The compound. The friends. The aunties. I thought of Ifeoma weeping on my shoulder, of Emeka's shaking hands, of the old man in the red cap.

"We don't know yet," he said, "which is why we need the funeral to happen. The body goes back into the coffin this afternoon, unexamined, unopened, and the funeral proceeds. Odogwu will be here. His men will be here. And the person who poured the cup will be here — they always come back to watch." He took a breath. "And when the time is right, you will blow the whistle."

I touched the whistle. "What will that do?"

"The old call," he said. "Three long blasts. Your grandmother taught you as a child. The hunters of this village — the men who keep the old customs, the ones who answer the keeper's call — they will come. And my team will close the roads." He looked at me. "But the whistle will only bring people if they believe the person blowing it is the keeper. Tonight, the whole village saw you leave the compound. They will see you again tomorrow at the funeral. Do you understand what I am asking you to do?"

"You're asking me to stand at my grandmother's funeral and smile at the man who killed her," I said.

"Yes."

"And not break his neck myself?"

"Yes." He almost smiled. "That part comes later."

Outside, the fire was being put out, and the voices of the village rose and fell, and somewhere at the edge of the field, the torches had gone out.

"One more thing," he said, as he turned to go. "The whistling you have been hearing at night. The tune."

I went cold. "You?"

"Part of it," he said. "My watchman near the grove whistled it, to unsettle the men who were digging. It worked — they believe the dead woman is whistling at them. Superstition is the only weapon we had down there." He paused. "But not all of it was him. The whistle I heard on the night your grandmother died — the whistle at the moment of her death — that was not my man. My man was two hundred meters away and asleep."

"What are you saying?"

"I am saying," he said, "that I do not know who whistled that night. And I do not like the things I do not know."

He left. I sat in the wreckage of my grandmother's room, with the whistle around my neck and the smell of smoke in my clothes, and I thought about a dead woman's whistle, and about the men who were digging, and about the cup of palm wine, and about who, in this compound of friends and family, had poured it.

## CHAPTER EIGHT: The Mission House

I did not sleep. I could not sleep. But I cleaned my room, and I put the mattress back, and I went to help the women, because a woman who does not help at a funeral is noticed, and I could not afford to be noticed.

The fire had taken the shed and nothing else. The village had put it out in time. But my grandmother's papers — forty-one years of school records, letters, the photographs of ten thousand students — were gone, turned to ash and smoke, and I stood in front of the blackened wreck and felt the loss like a physical thing, and I made myself a promise: *the story would not burn with them.*

In the afternoon, the coffin came back from the mortuary. I saw the van arrive, and I saw the undertakers carry the coffin back into the parlor, and I saw Uncle Emeka standing on the veranda with his hands in his pockets, watching, and I saw something on his face — something I could not read, and I filed it away in the ledger of my mind, next to his shaking hands and his refusal to open the coffin and his sudden agreement to bury her fast.

The funeral was tomorrow. Everything was happening tomorrow.

At dusk, I went to the mango grove and found Nnamdi. He had hidden the box well — wrapped in his shirt, buried under the fallen trunk where we had hidden as children, covered with leaves.

"You fit keep am?" he asked.

"I have to keep it," I said. "But first — I need to see the mission house."

He stared at me. "You wan kill yourself."

"I need to see it," I said. "I need to know they're real. I need to see the girl from the bus with my own eyes, so that when I smile at the funeral tomorrow, I will know what I am smiling for."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he picked up a cutlass from the ground. "If we die, na your grave we go share."

The mission house was an hour's walk through the bush, along the old path that led to the stream and beyond, to the abandoned road the British had built and the forest had reclaimed. It was a relic — a two-story colonial building, its roof half gone, its windows blind, its walls covered in the green fur of neglect. The village children said it was haunted by the ghosts of the civil war. The village adults said the same thing, with less laughter.

There were lights in the windows.

Low lights, dimmed — the kind of light you use when you do not want to be seen from the road. And there was a sound: a low, steady hum — a generator, running behind the building, muffled under a tarp. And there was a car — the black SUV — parked under the trees at the side, covered with cut branches.

We crawled through the tall grass to the back wall. A board had come loose on a ground-floor window — the kind of small neglect that killers always leave somewhere — and I pressed my face to the gap, and I saw them.

Five girls. On the floor of a bare room, on mattresses, wrapped in thin blankets. The girl from the bus was one of them. She was sitting up, her back against the wall, her eyes open, staring at nothing — the eyes of a person who has stopped expecting anything. Beside her, a smaller girl — twelve, maybe, no more — was asleep with her head on the older girl's shoulder.

And moving among them, carrying a tray of cups, was the woman in lace.

She was smiling. That same fixed, professional smile. The smile of a woman whose smile is her job.

I watched for a long time. I watched the woman set down the cups. I watched her pat the smallest girl's head, and I watched the girl flinch, and I watched the woman's smile not change by a millimeter. And I watched a man come in — young, with a machete at his belt — and speak to the woman, and the woman looked toward the window where I was hidden, and for one terrible second I thought she had seen me, and my whole body went rigid—

And then she looked away, and walked out of the room, and the man followed.

Nnamdi tugged my sleeve. "Come," he whispered. "Before dem come back."

I did not move. I was watching the girl from the bus. She had turned her head toward the window — toward the gap — and she was looking at the darkness, at the place where I was, as if she could feel me there. And I saw her lips move. A word. I could not hear it, but I could read it, the way you read a word on a drowning person's lips:

*"Aunty."*

The girl from the bus remembered me. She remembered the woman who had come down the aisle of the bus, three rows from the back, and said, *If you need anything — anything at all — I'm three rows back.*

I had been three rows back. And then I had gotten off the bus, and I had let the black SUV take her, because I did not know yet what I knew now.

I turned away. Nnamdi and I crawled back through the grass, and I did not speak until we were deep in the bush, and then I stopped, and I looked at him, and I said:

"She remembered me."

"So?"

"So if anything goes wrong tomorrow," I said, "I want you to know something. My grandmother did not die for nothing. And those girls are not going to die at all. Do you understand me?"

He looked at my face. "I understand you," he said. "But na the person wey understand you wey go dey bury you, so take am easy."

We walked back to the compound in the dark, and the compound was quiet, and I went to the veranda to wash my feet at the tap — and Uncle Emeka was sitting there.

He was not drinking. That was the first thing I noticed. My uncle, who drank palm wine from noon, was sitting on the veranda step with his hands between his knees, sober as a church bell, and he had been waiting for me.

"Sit down," he said. "Please."

I sat. The night was warm, and the crickets were loud, and neither of us spoke for a long moment.

"Chidi," he said at last, "I know say you think say I be part of the people wey kill your grandmother."

I did not deny it.

"Na so e dey look," he said. "I know. But I no kill am. I swear to God, I no kill am. She be my mother. The woman wey born me." His voice broke, and he put his hand over his mouth, and he was quiet for a moment, and then he went on: "But I know who carry the cup."

I went very still.

"Na Ifeoma," he said. "I see am. The night she die. I no fit sleep, I dey outside, I see Ifeoma come out from the parlor side, dey carry small cup. She see me. She stop. She look at me. And she talk. She talk say: 'Emeka, if you talk, your son go die. You hear me? Your son wey dey boarding school for Enugu. We know the bus he dey take. We know the time. If you talk, e go be the last time you see am.'"

His hands were shaking. "Chidi, I sign the paper. The deed. The one wey say the land don belong to Green Horizon. I sign am because of my son. Because Odogwu say if I no sign, my boy go follow the other girls. You understand? My boy — Ebere — e dey twelve. E dey small. E no know wetin im papa don do."

I looked at my uncle — the man who had refused to open the coffin, the man who had wanted to bury her fast, the man whose hands had been shaking at nine in the morning — and I understood, at last, that the shakes had not been guilt.

They had been terror.

"Ifeoma," I said slowly. "Aunty Ifeoma. She was Mama's closest friend. They grew up together. She was the one crying loudest at the coffin."

"Na the true tears," Emeka said. "She love Mama. She really love am. But Odogwu hold her daughter. Since twenty years. Ifeoma's daughter — Chioma — na the first girl wey Odogwu take from this village. When Ifeoma try to report am, Odogwu show am say 'you be the one wey help am find work for Lagos.' He make am dey part of the matter. Since then, na she dey find the girls for am. She dey smile, she dey cry, she dey find girls. Twenty years."

I sat in the dark, on the veranda step, with my uncle's confession around me like a cold cloth, and I thought about the woman who had wept on my shoulder — *"My sister's daughter has come"* — and about the woman on the bus — the fixed smile — and about a girl named Chioma, taken twenty years ago, whose mother had been made to become the thing that took her.

"Do you know where Chioma is?" I asked.

Emeka shook his head. "Nobody know. Ifeoma don't talk am. She dey find am since twenty years. Na the same thing wey dey keep am dey go." He looked at me. "You go stop am?"

"Yes," I said.

"How?"

"I don't know yet," I said. "But tomorrow is the funeral. And everybody comes to the funeral."

## CHAPTER NINE: The Funeral

The day of the funeral came up red over the iroko tree, like an eye opening slowly.

I dressed carefully. A black wrapper, a black blouse, a black headtie. I put the whistle around my neck, under my clothes, where the metal lay flat and cold against my chest, and I put the diary in my bra, and I looked at myself in the small mirror on my grandmother's wall, and I practiced the face I would need.

Not the face of a woman whose grandmother was poisoned.

The face of a woman at a funeral. Grief, yes. A little numbness. And gratitude for the people who had come to honor the dead.

The compound filled with mourners from early morning. The women sang as they worked; the men sat in clusters under the awnings, passing kola nuts and palm wine; the children ran between the chairs. And everywhere, the talk was the same: the old woman had died in her sleep, what a pity, may her soul rest. The word *witch* had gone back underground, where it lived when the elders were not around to feed it.

The priest arrived at nine — a young man, Rev. Father Umeh, with a kind face and nervous hands, who had been given his instructions by the council, and who kept glancing at the closed coffin as if it made him uncomfortable.

And at ten, the convoy came up the road.

I heard it before I saw it — the low growl of engines, the crunch of tires on red earth. Four black SUVs, moving slowly, the way power moves: with no need to hurry. They parked at the junction, and the doors opened, and the men got out — big men, in dark suits that did not fit the village and never meant to.

And then the center car opened, and Chief Odogwu stepped out.

I had seen his face in photographs — in newspapers, at foundation launches, grinning next to governors, cutting ribbons. He was in his sixties, with a face like a drum — broad, smooth, well-fed — and a white agbada that must have cost more than this village earned in a month. He carried a staff in one hand, and he moved through the crowd with the unhurried confidence of a man who owns the ground he walks on, and people parted for him the way water parts for a boat.

He did not come alone. Behind him, in the second car, was the woman in lace.

I saw her, and my heart went cold, and I understood: *she has come to watch.* To watch the coffin go into the ground. To watch the deed get signed. To make sure the old woman's mouth stayed shut.

And behind them — walking separately, in black, carrying a basket of oranges as a condolence gift — came Ifeoma, her face arranged in the mask of the faithful friend, ready to wail at the graveside.

Odogwu came to me first. Of course he did. The granddaughter from Lagos, the one who wrote for the papers — he would want to see my face, to measure me, to file me away.

"Chidinma," he said, and his voice was warm, and his hands were soft, and he took my hands in his and looked into my eyes with the practiced sincerity of a man who has looked into a thousand grieving faces. "My heart is with you. Your grandmother was a mother to this village. She taught half of us to read. When I heard, I wept." He pressed my hands. "If there is anything — anything at all — you must ask."

I looked into his eyes. And I smiled the funeral smile, and I said: "Thank you, Chief. She would have been honored that you came."

And I thought: *I am going to take everything from you. I am going to take your girls, and your land, and your senator, and your life, and I am going to do it at my grandmother's funeral, because she taught me that the dead do not forgive, and neither do I.*

The service was at the church — St. Michael's, white-walled, packed to the doors. The choir sang, and the women wept, and Rev. Father Umeh preached about the life of a teacher, and the coffin stood at the altar with the photograph of my grandmother smiling on top of it, and I sat in the front row and watched Odogwu dab at his eyes with a white handkerchief.

Then the burial procession. The coffin on the shoulders of six men, carried through the village, past the market, past the stream, to the graveside at the edge of the compound, under the old gmelina trees. The grave had been dug the day before — a red gash in the red earth — and the earth from it stood in a mound, and the priest stood at the head of the grave, and the mourners ringed it three deep.

And at the graveside, I saw them all, arranged like pieces on a board:

Odogwu, standing at the back, by the tree, with his men around him. The woman in lace, at the edge, watching. Ifeoma, in the front row, weeping into a handkerchief, her shoulders shaking. Ozo Igweoke, with his carved stick, at the head of the elders. Uncle Emeka, pale, sober, gripping the back of a chair until his knuckles went white. And Nnamdi, at the edge of the crowd, with four of his friends, their cutlasses hidden in the folds of their wrappers.

And the priest lifted his hands, and the prayers began, and the coffin was lowered into the grave.

I heard the ropes. I heard the wood scrape the walls of the grave. I heard the first handful of red earth hit the coffin with a sound like rain on a tin roof — and I stepped forward.

"Wait," I said.

The whole village turned. The priest's hands froze in the air. The women stopped weeping to look.

"Wait," I said again. "It is the custom. Before the earth covers her — the grandchildren must look upon her face. One last time. It is our custom. It has always been our custom."

A murmur ran through the crowd. The elders stirred. And Ozo Igweoke stepped forward, his stick planted in the earth like a flag.

"Chidinma," he said, with the patience of a man explaining a rule to a child, "the body has been sealed. It is not good—"

"It is our custom," I said. "The grandchildren look upon the face. Do you refuse the custom, Ozo? In front of the whole village?"

The silence was total. In a village, you can fight many things — the chief, the priest, the government — but you cannot fight the custom, because the custom is the village. And Ozo Igweoke knew it. His jaw worked. He looked, for one moment, at Odogwu — a look that lasted half a second, but I saw it — and then he nodded.

"Open it," he said. "Quickly."

The men pulled the coffin back up. The ropes strained. The coffin came over the lip of the grave and was set down on the earth, and I walked to it, and I lifted the lid.

My grandmother's face. Peaceful. The blue tinge at her lips, dark against the candle-pale skin. And I reached in, the way I had reached in at midnight in the parlor, and I found the hem of her wrapper — the inner hem, where the seam had been left loose, the way a woman leaves a seam loose when she is sewing a secret — and I worked my fingers into it, and I felt it: a photograph, folded, hidden inside the cloth.

The body had been the message. All along, the body had been the message. They had searched her house, her tree, her compound — but the body, the one thing they would never dare to touch, the body had carried it.

I lifted the photograph out, and I unfolded it, and I looked at it.

Two men, shaking hands, in front of a hotel in Abuja. One was Odogwu. The other — older, grayer, in a senator's cap — was Senator Ozoani.

And at that exact moment, behind me, the woman in lace screamed.

Not a cry — a signal. And the crowd exploded.

Odogwu's men moved like water — sudden, everywhere — and the mourners scattered like chickens, and I saw the woman in lace pushing through the crowd toward me, her fixed smile gone, her face for the first time real: a face of pure, open hatred.

And I saw Odogwu. He was not moving toward me. He was moving away — toward the bush, toward the iroko tree, toward the old path to the mission house — the path a man uses when his car is not where he left it, because Adebayo's team had taken the cars.

He was running.

And I ran after him.

"Chidi!" — Nnamdi's voice — but I did not stop. I had the photograph in one hand and the whistle in the other, and I ran through the scattering crowd, past the church, past the compound fence, into the tall grass, after the man who had killed my grandmother.

He was fast for his age. The bush path was narrow, and the branches whipped my face, and behind me I heard the whole village in an uproar — screams, shouts, the flat crack of what might have been a gunshot — but I did not look back.

The iroko tree rose ahead of us, vast and black in the morning light, its roots like the knuckles of a buried giant. And Odogwu reached it, and he stopped, and he turned, and in his hand there was a gun.

"Come closer," he said, "and I will put you in the ground next to your grandmother."

I stopped. The tree stood between us. The morning was bright, and the shadows of the leaves moved on the ground like water, and I stood twenty feet from a man with a gun, holding the photograph that would hang him.

"Give me the photograph," he said.

"No."

"You think I am the only one? You think this ends with me?" He laughed — a wet, breathless laugh. "There are men in Abuja who will burn this village to the ground before they let that photograph reach a court. Give it to me, girl. I will make it quick."

"You poisoned her," I said.

"She was going to destroy everything," he said. "Two years she was feeding names to that policeman. Two years I let her live, because killing a beloved old woman in a village like this brings trouble. And then she found the list —" He gestured at the ground, at the roots, at the place where the box had been buried. "She hid it under my own feet. Do you know how many nights my men dug this ground, looking for what she took? She laughed at us. From the grave, she laughed at us." His eyes went hard. "The whistle. She taught you the whistle. Do you think I don't know what that whistle means in this village?"

"Then you know the hunters will come," I said.

"They will not come for you," he said. "They will come for the keeper. And you —" He raised the gun. "— you are not the keeper. You are a journalist from Lagos who does not belong here."

And that was when I understood what he was really afraid of.

Not the police. Not the court. Not the photograph.

The whistle. The keeper. The old world — the world of the grove and the third root and the women's land, the world he had been quietly burying with the deed of Green Horizon Farms.

I lifted the whistle to my lips.

"Don't," he said. But his voice had lost its edge.

I blew.

Three long blasts. The sound flew over the grove, over the stream, over the whole village — the sound my grandmother had blown to call me in from the bush at dusk, the sound the children called *the whistle that brings the moon.*

And for a moment, nothing moved.

And then the bush around the iroko tree came alive.

They came out of the tall grass with lanterns that burned in daylight — old men in raffia masks, young men with cutlasses, hunters in leather caps with Dane guns over their shoulders. The Ogbuefi — the guardians of the grove, the ones who had answered the keeper's call since before the church came, since before the mission, since before the memory of the village itself. They ringed the tree in silence, and the silence was worse than any shout.

Odogwu's gun wavered. "You— you do not know what you have done," he said. "I own this village. I own your senator. I own your police. I own—"

"You do not own this tree," I said. "You never owned this tree. That is the thing you never understood about my grandmother. She did not fight you with a gun. She fought you with a deed from 1911, and a diary, and a whistle, and forty-one years of teaching the children of this village to read. And that—" I held up the photograph. "—is the end of you."

The gun was still pointed at my chest. But I watched his hand, and I saw what the guardians' silence had done to it: the tremor, the sweat, the small telltale shaking of a man who has finally run out of ground.

I took one step forward.

Then a second.

Then a third —

And the gun went off.

The sound was enormous — it cracked the morning like an egg — and I felt the wind of the bullet pass my ear, and behind me someone shouted my name, and Odogwu's face twisted, and he swung the gun toward the guardians — and that was when Uncle Emeka came out of the grass.

My uncle, the drunk, the coward, the man who had signed the deed — he came out of the grass like a released animal, and he hit Odogwu from the side, and they went down together, and the gun went off again — a wild shot into the leaves — and the guardians were on them in a flood of raffia and muscle, and I heard Odogwu scream, and I heard Emeka cry out — a short, sharp sound, like a dog —

"Uncle!"

They pulled them apart. Odogwu was pinned under five men, his white agbada torn, his face in the red earth, and the gun was kicked away into the grass. And Emeka lay on his back, holding his shoulder, and the blood was coming through his fingers, dark and fast, and he looked up at me, and he smiled — my uncle, the coward, smiled.

"Na me dey carry am now," he said. "The bullet wey Odogwu shoot for you. Na im dey for my shoulder." He laughed, and coughed. "Chidi... tell Ebere say im papa no be coward again."

"Uncle—" I knelt beside him, pressing my hands on the wound. "Uncle, you are not going to die. You hear me? You are not—"

And around us, the whole village arrived — the mourners, the elders, the women in their black wrappers — and behind them, running, came Adebayo, with his warrant card out and his men behind him, and I saw the woman in lace in handcuffs, her face empty, and I saw Ifeoma — weeping, real tears now, the first real ones in twenty years — and I saw the girl from the bus.

She was walking across the field. In the daylight. Not running. Walking, as if she had forgotten how to run, with a policewoman's arm around her shoulders, and behind her, four more girls, blinking in the sun like people coming out of a long, dark room.

And I looked at my grandmother's tree — the iroko, the keeper's tree, the tree under which the men had dug for three nights looking for what they could not find — and I swear to you, I saw the wind move through its branches, though there was no wind.

And I heard it.

Just once, from the direction of the graveside.

Three long blasts.

The whistle that brings the moon.

## CHAPTER TEN: The Keeper's Eulogy

The postmortem was released a week later, and the paper said it plainly: *"The deceased died of acute chloroquine poisoning, administered in food or drink within hours of death."*

Chloroquine. The bitter anti-malaria medicine, dissolved in palm wine. Enough to stop an old woman's heart in her sleep, with no marks, no struggle — nothing but blue lips, if you knew to look.

The village read the report, and the whispers of *witch* died in the mouths that had made them. They died because the girls came home, and everyone could see the marks on their wrists, and everyone could hear the story of the mission house, and everyone could see that the old woman who had been called a witch had been, all along, the one who had kept the names.

Fourteen names were on the list. Fourteen girls. And one by one, they came back:

Blessing, the girl from the bus, returned to her mother in Awka, and her mother fainted in the police station, and Blessing stood there, holding her mother's hand, saying nothing, for a long time.

The four girls from the mission house went home to four different villages, and the women of those villages learned, at last, that the old teacher from Oda had been fighting for them.

And in Lagos, in a safehouse in Festac — the one Adebayo's team raided the same morning as the funeral, the one that had been watched for eight months — they found Adaeze.

Not the girl from the bus. The fourth name on the list — the one my grandmother had crossed out and written twice — found at last.

She was twenty-eight years old. She had been taken when she was sixteen, and somewhere in the twelve years, the girl had become a woman who no longer expected anything, and who smiled only when the matron wasn't looking. The officers said she walked out of the safehouse into the Lagos sun, and she looked up at the sky, and she stood there for a very long time, blinking.

Her mother — my grandmother's friend, the one whose wrapper, whose smile, whose friendship Ifeoma had worn like a stolen coat — her mother was in the village when the news came. She walked to the compound, and she sat down on the veranda step, and she cried without making a sound, and then she stood up and walked to the graveside, and she stood at the fresh mound of red earth, and she said:

"Nne Nnenna. You kept your word. You kept my child's name in your book, and now my child is coming home. Thank you. Thank you for not letting them bury you with the truth."

The senator was arrested in Abuja on a Tuesday morning, at his house, in his cap, in his bathrobe, and the photograph that did it — the two men shaking hands in front of the hotel — was printed on the front page of every paper in the country, above the headline: *THE KEEPER OF THE GROVE.*

The trial would take years. The country is like that. But the story was out, and the story does not go back in the bottle, and somewhere in a cell, a man in a torn white agbada was learning what the village already knew: you can buy a lot of things in this country, but you cannot buy the memory of an old woman who kept a diary.

As for the funeral — the real funeral — we held it properly, one week later, at the compound.

The coffin was closed this time, but there was no shame in the closing. The village knew the face behind it, and the blue lips had become a badge of honor: *this is the woman who died with the evidence sewn into her hem.*

The church was full. Rev. Father Umeh preached, and the choir sang, and the women wailed their true wails, and then it was my turn.

I stood at the lectern, in my black wrapper, with the whistle around my neck — I had stopped hiding it — and I looked out at the faces: the elders, the aunties, the hunters with their cutlasses laid across their knees, Nnamdi with his arm in a sling from the fire, Emeka in a wheelchair at the back, his shoulder bandaged, tears running down his face without shame.

"My grandmother was not a witch," I said. "She was a guard. She guarded this stream, and she guarded this grove, and she guarded the deed from 1911 that says this land belongs to the women of this village, and she guarded the names of fourteen girls whose names the police did not have time to write down.

"She was a teacher for forty-one years. She taught this village to read. And when she saw that the people who were taking our daughters could not be stopped by the police, or the courts, or the men who own the land — she did the only thing a teacher knows how to do.

"She kept records.

"She kept a list.

"She kept a whistle — the whistle her own mother gave her, and her mother's mother before that — the whistle that says, *when you hear this sound, the keeper is in trouble, and the village must come.*

"And on the night she died, she called me, and she said: *'If I die before you come home, do not let them put me in the ground.'*

"She did not say it because she was afraid of death. She said it because she knew the body would be searched — the house, the tree, the ground — and she knew they would never dare to search the body itself. The body was the message. The photograph was in the hem of her wrapper. And the whistle —" I touched it. "— the whistle was the key.

"So I stand here today to tell you what a witch really is. A witch is a woman who knows things she is not supposed to know, and says them anyway. By that definition —" I smiled, and the tears came, and I let them. "— my grandmother was the most powerful witch this village has ever produced. And I am proud to be her granddaughter."

I lifted the whistle to my lips, and I blew.

Three long blasts.

And the hunters rose, and lifted their Dane guns to the sky, and fired — a volley that rolled over the compound like thunder, the old salute, the salute for a keeper of the grove.

And somewhere, at the back of the crowd, I swear I heard it — a whistle, answering.

## EPILOGUE: Dawn

I stayed in the village for one month.

I helped sort my grandmother's things. I gave her books to the school. I hung her photograph — the one of her at twenty-two, holding the chalk like a rifle — in the new library they named after her, the Mama Nnenna Okafor Reading Room, in the building that used to be the mission house.

The mission house. The girls were gone; the lights were gone; the generator was gone. The village children say it is haunted still, but by a different kind of ghost now — the ghost of a hundred girls who passed through it and survived, and who will tell their daughters, one day, about the old woman in the village who kept their names in a book.

The grove was returned to the women of Oda Village. The deed from 1911 hangs in a glass frame in the town hall, and the land of the iroko tree — the tree that is older than the church, older than the mission, older than the memory of the village — belongs to nobody, and to everybody, and the men who wanted to plant it with progress went away, and did not come back.

Odogwu is in prison, awaiting trial, and the senator is in prison, awaiting trial, and the woman in lace is in prison, awaiting trial, and the case has a name now — the reporters call it *the Keeper of the Grove case* — and I have been called to testify, and I will, gladly, every time.

Ifeoma is also awaiting trial. She did not fight it. When they came for her, she was sitting on the floor of her room, with a photograph of her daughter — a photograph from twenty years ago, a school photograph, the girl with the gap-toothed smile — and she handed it to the policewoman, and she said: *"Find her. That is all I ask. Find her and tell her I am sorry."*

They found her, eventually. Not in a safehouse. In a clinic in Kano, where a woman with no memory of her name had been living for nine years, working as a cleaner, cared for by nuns. They are bringing her home. The mother who was made to become the thing that took her daughter will never hold her daughter again — not the way she dreams — but the daughter is alive, and alive is a place you can build from.

Uncle Emeka is healing. The bullet took a piece of his shoulder and gave him back something else — his name, maybe, or his sobriety. He drinks only at ceremonies now, and when he drinks, he tells the story of the iroko tree, and his voice is steady, and his son Ebere sits at his feet and looks at him the way a boy looks at a man he is proud of.

Nnamdi went back to school. He called me from the campus of the University of Nigeria, Nsukka, one evening, and he said: *"Chidi, I dey read law now. The kind law wey go take back the land from people like Odogwu. Na wetin your grandmother go want."*

I said: *"She would want you to pass your exams, first."*

He laughed. Then he said, quieter: *"The whistle wey you carry. You still get am?"*

"Yes," I said.

"Good," he said. "Keep am. Because na so the keeper dey continue."

I went back to Lagos. I wrote the story — the whole story, fourteen names, one whistle, one tree — and it ran on the front page, and it was syndicated, and it is being made into something, some producer called, and I told him to send the money to the reading room in Oda, and he did.

And on the last morning before I left the village — the morning I was to take the bus back to Enugu, and the plane back to Lagos — I walked to the iroko tree at dawn.

The mist was rising off the stream. The tree stood in it like a great dark cathedral, its roots buried in the red earth, and the ground where the men had dug was smooth now, healed over, the grass growing back the way grass always grows back.

I stood under the tree, and I took the whistle from around my neck, and I held it in my hand, warm from my skin, and I thought about my grandmother: the way she called me in at dusk, the way she laughed, the way she said I was a difficult baby and loved me anyway, the way she fought for forty-one years with nothing but a chalkboard and a whistle and a list of names.

I raised the whistle to my lips.

I blew three long blasts.

The sound flew over the grove, over the stream, over the village, and the mist moved, and the leaves stirred, and for one moment — one long, bright moment — I thought I saw, at the edge of the grove, a tall figure in a wrapper and a headtie, standing very still, nodding.

Then the sun came up over the tree, and the mist thinned, and there was nothing there but the morning.

I put the whistle around my neck, and I walked back to the compound, and I got my bag, and I left.

The dead do not whistle. That is what the people in the city will tell you, and they are right, and they are wrong, and I do not argue with them anymore.

Some things you do not explain. Some things you only carry.

I carry the whistle. I carry the diary. I carry the names.

And when dusk comes, wherever I am, I take the whistle out, and I blow it once — soft, not calling anyone — and I listen.

And sometimes — I hear her answering.
