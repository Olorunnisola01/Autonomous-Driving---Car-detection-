It was a crumpled bus ticket, folded into the wallet of a girl who had been rescued by the road near Lagos—Abeokuta before she could be taken out of the country in a container. On the back of the ticket, in handwriting that was not hers, was a phone number. I had called it from a prepaid SIM. A man had picked up on the second ring and said one word: "Gbakwa?" — hang up — and the line had gone dead. I knew that voice. I had written about him in my third article. The man they called Odogwu. So when my phone buzzed at 3:47 a.m. and I saw MAMA NNENNA on the screen, I almost didn't answer. The only people who call at that hour are the dead and the dying, and people in trouble. My grandmother was seventy-eight years old, living alone in the family compound in Oda Village, Enugu State, three hundred miles and one lifetime away from Surulere. She did not own a smartphone.
