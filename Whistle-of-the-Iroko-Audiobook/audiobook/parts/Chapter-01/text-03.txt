She owned a Nokia that rang like a sewing machine and that she charged only on Sundays. "Grandma," I said. "Kedu. It is 3:47 in the morning— " "Chidi, listen to me." Her voice was not the slow, honeyed voice I remembered from childhood — the voice that sang to me when she ground pepper, the voice that told me the story of the tortoise and the drum. This voice was quick, and tight, and it came down the line like a person walking a very narrow bridge. "They are digging under the iroko tree. They have been digging for three nights. I have heard them." I rubbed my eyes. "Who, Grandma? Who is digging?" "The ones who come at night. The ones in the black car." A pause. I heard her breathing, and behind her breathing, the wind — the thick, wet wind of the East, heavy with rain that had not yet fallen. "And they are not the only ones. There is a woman from the village who has gone with them.
